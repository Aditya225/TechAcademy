What Real Career Counseling for Developers Actually Looks Like

By Techs Academy · Published on September 2026 · 5 min read

Ask most students what career counseling means, and you'll get the same answer: "They ask you what your interests are, then tell you a field." That's not counseling — that's a survey. Real counseling starts where the interest conversation ends.

Interest tells you what a student thinks they want. It doesn't tell you what they're actually good at, how they behave under pressure, or whether they can work with a team when things go wrong. That gap is exactly where most students get misdirected — and exactly where real counseling has to work.

1. Start With an Honest Skill Assessment

Before any conversation about "which field is right for you," we run a mock test — not to grade the student, but to see where they genuinely stand. A resume or a course certificate tells you what someone has studied. A live assessment tells you what they can actually do with it.

This step alone removes most of the guesswork. A student who thinks they're "average at backend" might turn out to have strong problem-solving instincts once tested properly — or the opposite. Either way, the conversation that follows is based on evidence, not assumption.

2. Test Problem-Solving in Real Situations, Not Just Code

Writing correct code in a quiet room is one skill. Figuring out what broke in production, under time pressure, with incomplete information, is a completely different one — and it's the one that actually matters on the job.

During 1-on-1 sessions, we deliberately walk students through scenarios like: "This API is throwing intermittent errors only during peak traffic — what do you check first?" How a student approaches ambiguity, what questions they ask, and whether they jump to conclusions or investigate systematically tells us far more than any coding test could.

3. Understand How They Work With a Team

A strong individual coder isn't automatically a strong team member. Counseling conversations explore how a student behaves when someone else's code is blocking their work, whether they explain their reasoning clearly to others, and whether they ask for help or quietly struggle.

This matters because companies aren't hiring a person to work alone — they're hiring someone to fit into a team that ships together. A student who is technically strong but avoids collaboration often struggles more in their first job than one who is slightly weaker technically but communicates well.

4. Help Them Find the Right Field — Not Just Any Field

Most students choose a specialization based on what's trending, not what fits them. The result: someone who's naturally strong at structured, logical problem-solving ends up forcing themselves into a field that rewards a completely different kind of thinking, and wonders why they're struggling.

The goal of this counseling isn't to hand a student a label. It's to combine what the assessment shows, how they handle real problems, and how they work with others — and use all three together to point toward where they're likely to genuinely thrive, not just where they can technically qualify.

Why This Approach Works Better

A single conversation about "interests" can be wrong in either direction — a student might undersell themselves out of nervousness, or oversell themselves out of pressure to sound confident. Testing across skill, real-world problem-solving, and teamwork removes that guesswork and gives a far more honest picture of where a student actually stands.
